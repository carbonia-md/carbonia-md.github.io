Carbonia was founded on 18 December 1938, by decree of Benito Mussolini's Fascist government, to house workers for the Sirai-Serbariu coalfield in Sardinia's Sulcis basin. It was built in under two years: a full town, not a mining camp, on ground that had no settlement of its own shortly before.

> **At a glance:** The town's mining workforce collapsed from roughly 14,000 to 5,000 between 1947 and 1957[^1]. Depending which source you read, its founding mine shut down anywhere from 1964 to 1971. Carbonia has outlived its original purpose by longer than the purpose itself lasted.

## A Company Town by Decree

The founding had a specific, dated cause: after Italy's invasion of Ethiopia in 1935-36, the League of Nations imposed sanctions that squeezed Italy's access to imported raw materials. Mussolini's government answered with a national push toward autarky (self-sufficiency), and Sardinian lignite, not especially high-grade but domestic, became strategically important almost overnight. Carbonia was born to mine it.

The town's layout announced its purpose. Public buildings were monumental and centralized around Piazza Roma and Piazza Impero (renamed Piazza Matteotti in 1944)[^3]: the municipal building, the Casa del Fascio, a church, and a civic tower, arranged in a plan meant to express order, discipline, and the centrality of political power, the standard vocabulary of Italy's other Fascist-era foundation towns.

## Life Underground

Wartime conditions turned already-hard mining work into something closer to forced labor. Miners worked three continuous shifts covering the full day, underground in damp, physically punishing conditions. Malaria was endemic across the Sulcis area, compounded by trachoma, tuberculosis, and anemia among the mining population[^2]. During the war, the prescribed bread ration for miners (200 grams a day) was, by contemporary accounts, grossly inadequate for the physical demands of the work, and malnourished miners, weakened further by cyclical malaria attacks, pushed for higher rations[^2]. Fascist-regime trade unions, meanwhile, deducted compulsory membership dues from wages that were already low. Wartime discipline treated any protest as sabotage. Yet between 1940 and 1943, Carbonia's miners struck anyway, in one of the first labor actions recorded anywhere in Italy under Fascism. That combativeness would remain a trait of Carbonia's people through later labor battles too.

## The Long Decline

After the war, Sardinian coal became less and less competitive. When Italy joined the European Coal and Steel Community in the 1950s, the industry entered a managed downsizing, and the mining workforce fell from about 14,000 to about 5,000 between 1947 and 1957 alone[^1]. Miners did not leave quietly: 1948 brought one of the longest strikes in Italian labor history, reported at 72 days, fought against the layoffs[^1].

Exactly when large-scale coal mining ended here is one of the few facts in this history that does not line up cleanly across sources. The Museo del Carbone, built on the former Serbariu mine site, dates that mine's operational life to 1937-1964; a separate account describes the nearby Seruci mine's closure in 1964 as well, following the sector's broader crisis, while other sources put final closure (or at least the last recorded activity) closer to 1971. What is not in dispute is the outcome: former miners were expected to find new work at the Enel power plant and the new Portovesme Industrial Hub, an aluminum-and-metals complex built on the same coastline. It could not absorb them all: a high capital-to-labor ratio meant the whole complex offered only 4,000 to 6,000 jobs against a much larger workforce shed by the mines. From the 1970s on, Carbonia's economy shifted toward services, and the region's "Piano di Rinascita" (Renaissance Plan) failed to solve the youth unemployment that followed.

## A Crisis Without an End, Amid Light and Shadow

Portovesme's own industrial base then hit the same wall a generation later. In the late 1990s, ENI's exit from Carbosulcis and Alcoa's abandonment of its aluminum plant were pivotal blows, driven by energy costs that could not compete on the European market and by inadequate logistics infrastructure. The 2008 global financial crisis compounded it: Eurallumina, the alumina plant at Portovesme, has been reported as idle since as early as 2009 by some accounts and only partially closed by 2012 in others. It is the same kind of date ambiguity that runs through this whole history, this time for the plant that was supposed to be part of the mines' replacement.

As of 2026, the industrial crisis remains unresolved, and the town's future is uncertain. Four major Portovesme-area facilities (the Enel power plant, Portovesme Srl, Eurallumina, and the former Alcoa, now Sider Alloys) together account for over a thousand jobs directly at risk, with roughly as many again in subcontracting. Around 300 workers remain in extended redundancy status with no clear path back to work. A government commitment made in September 2025 to convene a follow-up meeting on the former Alcoa plant by December was never honored.

![View of the former mine site from Monte Rosmarino](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/da/Miniera_Serbariu_da_monte_Rosmarino.jpg)

The mine is still visible today from [[pineta-di-monte-rosmarino|Monte Rosmarino]], the pine park that now serves as the town's everyday green space for its residents. Carbonia's mines closed decades ago. Its economic aftermath has not.

## The Will to Keep Fighting

Despite a difficult history, Carbonia is still here, and even accounting for its demographic and employment decline, it remains one of the most important urban centers in the Sulcis (it is co-capital, along with Iglesias, of the Province of Sulcis Iglesiente[^4]). Today Carbonia is an important service hub for the area and a reference point for the many tourists who choose the Sulcis's scenic coastline for their holidays.
